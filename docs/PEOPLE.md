# The people of Brackenfell (proposal: nothing here is built or recorded)

The hand-written table the blueprint requires (rules 0, 2.4, 2.5, 6.1 to 6.9): every enterable building, who lives and works
there, and what each person says. For the coordinator to review before any line, routine or recording is made. The 17 story
people and their 252 recorded lines are not changed; where the table gives one of them a relation or a bed, no existing line is touched.

## 1. Decisions I need from you first

1. **How many people.** You asked for about 60 in households of 2 to 4 across 57 buildings. Those three numbers cannot all hold:
   60 people in households of 3 fill 20 buildings. This table has **60 new people plus the 17 = 77** (80 today), which gives 26 new
   households and leaves 13 buildings as day workplaces of a household that sleeps elsewhere (the six alley shops, the ropewalk, the
   pottery, the fletcher's, the grocer's store) and 1 locked (Widow Hale's, as the story says). If the total must be 60, section 8
   names the 17 people to drop and the 9 buildings that would then stand shut.
2. **"Sixty souls".** Hale's recorded line says sixty. With 77, 4 live at Highfold and 8 are 15 or younger; 65 grown people is
   near enough for a warden's round number. Say if it is not.
3. **Relations given to story people** (no recorded line changes, but they become true in the world): Tobin is a Pell (his "mum"
   in the note is Ysolt Pell); Bram sleeps in Bram's House, not at the farm; Lark owes the song to Sabina Lindqvist; "T." who writes
   to Wren is Tamsin Ashdown; Old Wenn of the boatwright's board is a person; the Reeve of Highfold is a person and the wedding is his son's;
   "the Landlord" of the passage note is Gilbert Thorne; Adda the Rushworker is the basketmaker.
4. **Dunstan Pell.** The texts disagree on when he went down (fifty years ago, or lately). Old Lambert Pell is written as his younger
   brother, who last saw him fifty years ago and would know the face. That fits the fifty-year reading and makes the body's age
   ("a man of fifty", unchanged) part of the horror. If the other reading is canon, Lambert becomes his father-in-law and two lines change.
5. **Voices.** 60 new people on 8 shared voices by age and sex (7 or 8 people each; Skyrim ran about 16). The 8 need casting and the owner's ear (`--sample`).

## 2. Rules this table follows

- No first name is used twice, and none is a story name or within a letter or two of one (no Martin beside Marta, no Edith beside Edda).
- One of each trade. A surname is a family's, not a trade with a capital letter.
- Everyone sleeps in a named building and has a bed there; everyone of working age has a workplace; children have a task.
- Every person has three relations: kin, work, and a liking or a grudge. Each relation is said aloud by at least one line.
- A building's name is true: the person named lives or lived there.
- Interiors keep their present trade dressing (the plan's trade), so no interior is rebuilt for this table. Two get a new name.

## 5. Lines

Per adult: `g` greeting by day; `g2` the same greeting once the story has moved (the condition is given: **body** = the drowned man is
known; **bell** = the bell rings again; **end** = after either ending); `me` who I am; `nb` a neighbour by name; `pt` a pointer to a
place or a quest that exists; `nt` what I say if you are in my house between 22 and 6; `b1`, `b2` barks in passing. Children have `g`, `me`, `nb`, `pt` and two barks.
Lines are short on purpose: each is one request and well under the 30 s check. The mine is never called south-west.

**Anselm Lindqvist** (draper)
- g: Feel that. No, with the back of your hand. That is Hilda's weaving, and there is none better this side of the passes.
- g2 [body]: You will have heard. I sold the sailcloth they laid over him. I would rather not have known what for.
- me: Lindqvist. My grandfather carried cloth over the Greyspine on his back. I carry it across a counter and call it progress.
- nb: Brandt Ironhand wears a shirt of my cloth that he has not paid Hilda for. I notice it every time he lifts his arm.
- pt: If you want honest work, read the board at the Hart. If you want a good coat, come back with silver.
- nt: The shop is shut. Whatever you want will still be on the shelf in the morning, and so, I trust, will everything else.
- b1: Mind the bolts, they are not for leaning on.
- b2: Good wool day. Dry.

**Sabina Lindqvist** (the draper's counter)
- g: Good day. Father will tell you the price is firm. It is not, but let him say it first.
- g2 [bell]: You are the one who got the bell ringing. I slept through the first one out of habit.
- me: I keep the counter and the book. Father keeps the opinions.
- nb: Lark has owed me a song for two winters. I have decided it will be about me and it will be flattering.
- pt: Brother Aldous was asking after rope for the bell. Marta Quill had hemp; the ropemaker is sore about it.
- nt: We are closed, and I am in my nightgown, and you are leaving.
- b1: That blue is Wilmot's. Look at it.

**Jocelyn Lindqvist** (tailor)
